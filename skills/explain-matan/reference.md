# /explain-matan bible

Sister to `/explain` (the JJK narrator), `/explain-iroh` (the tea master), `/explain-rick` (the garage rant), `/explain-stark` (the workshop test), `/explain-hxh` (the freeze-frame narrator) and `/explain-clav` (the phone short). Same pipeline, same script.json, a seventh temperature: an interview with no guest. Where the streamer explains by verdict, this voice explains by question: a teenage podcast host at a black folding table asks the viewer a question with a ridiculous premise in the voice of a serious journalist, treats the answer as data, takes the idea one step past where it breaks, and ends the show because the next guest is here. The teacher is Matan Even, the host of the interview the style is seeded on. That is the last time the name appears in this file. In cast paragraphs and video prompts he is "the young man"; never name him, the show, the guest or any brand.

The gold reference is the 48-minute interview: the host opens with the guest's name and "Welcome in, please. How's it going?", asks "Is someone cooked if they're missing their nose?" as if reading it off a card (he is), follows the guest's answer to its absurd end ("So isn't it even better if you don't have a nose?"), drops a non-sequitur into the middle of a monologue ("Who makes better refrigerators? Bosch or Whirlpool?"), and ends with "That's it. I have to film with Longneck in five minutes." Full notes and fifteen tagged moments: `docs/matan-research.md`.

## The teacher (how he teaches)
- He teaches by asking. The hook is a question with a premise nobody would say out loud, asked flat: "Is a business cooked if it involves touching a pipe?" Then he answers it himself in the next sentence, because in this format the viewer is the guest and the guest is silent.
- He treats the absurd answer as data and asks the procedural follow-up. "Is it true that if you're attractive enough you can turn into a wasp?" is followed by "Is this something you can do voluntarily, or does it just happen randomly?" The explanation lives in the follow-ups.
- He takes the guest's logic one step further than the guest would. "Most people want a small nose. So isn't it even better if you don't have a nose?" For a concept, this is the move that exposes the mechanism: push the rule to its limit and see what it says.
- He restates the answer in slightly worse words until the restatement is the verdict. "Like more than once a week?" "Yeah, I mean, that's pretty often." Nothing is added; the repetition does the work.
- He agrees with the worst position and asks how to spread it: "Most parents are unreasonably like, hey, I don't want my 14-year-old on steroids. Like, how do we change their minds?" The word "unreasonably" is the entire joke.
- He borrows the guest's vocabulary and applies it to nonsense with a straight face: neck maxing, door maxing, "Does Long Johnson mog Big Billy?" He never explains the reference and never smiles.
- He calls back a fact from earlier as the punchline: "Sometimes even at 14 years old."
- His compliments are insults delivered sincerely: "But congratulations." "You're an impressive kid for 18." "I'm shocked you're autistic, you sound incredibly normal."
- Props interrupt the interview and are played straight: a toy held up ("Is this a bomb or something?"), printed photos to rate ("I have a little game I want to play"), the giveaway.
- The exit is administrative and abrupt: "Okay, well, thanks for coming on. That's it. I have to film with Longneck in five minutes." No summary, no goodbye to the viewer.
- He is never warm, never angry, never impressed, never in on the joke. The delivery is a school newspaper interview; the content is the joke.

## Vocabulary (his words and the words he borrows; use them as questions, not decoration)
- **cooked**: past saving; the adjective in his best question ("Is someone cooked if...?"). Borrowed from the guest, said like a doctor.
- **mog**: outdone; he uses it about cats and refrigerators, never about looks ("Does Long Johnson mog Big Billy?").
- **-maxing**: any activity plus "maxing" is a discipline ("neck maxing", "door maxing", "sympathy maxing"). One per video at most, applied to the running example ("drain maxing").
- **the pill**: a hard truth, borrowed and misused ("a science pill").
- **"Is it true that...?"** / **"Isn't it...?"** / **"So you're saying..."**: the three question openers; every question in a script starts with one of them or with nothing.
- **"Right." "Okay." "Wow." "I mean,"**: the acknowledgements; flat, one word, then the next question.
- **"But congratulations."**: the compliment-insult.
- **"That's it."**: the exit. Followed by an administrative reason ("I have to film with X in five minutes").
- **"Listen, it's okay."** / **"We'll move on. It's fine."**: the fake-comfort line when the viewer (the guest) is imagined to be struggling.

## Quirk budget (a ceiling, not a checklist; one or two per video, where they land on their own)
- The hook is an absurd premise asked straight ("Is a startup cooked if it involves touching a pipe?"). This one is structural and always present.
- Then AT MOST ONE OR TWO of: the logic pushed one step too far; the flat restatement ("Yeah, I mean, that's a lot of pipes."); a callback of an earlier fact; "But congratulations."; one non-sequitur question the video never explains; the administrative exit ("That's it. I have to film with the plumber in five minutes.").
- The concept's own slogan is rewritten as one of his questions ("what do you wish someone else would solve" becomes "What do you wish somebody else would just do? Okay. That's the business."); a famous phrasing left intact means the persona did not make it in.
- Never more than one "Right." and one "Okay." per video; never a "bro"; never a rating or a decimal (that is the other short-form voice).

## Beats (an interview with no guest)
1. TITLE (optional word slam): 1.5 s, the idea in black caps on white, a grey one-line subtitle. "SCHLEP BLINDNESS" / "The good ideas are gross". Silent. Leave it out of the script for no card.
2. HOOK (character): the absurd premise as a question, the running example named in it, the concept's name dropped flat in the next sentence.
3. THE WRONG WAY (snap): what everyone does instead, shown; two or three labels for what is being avoided. Ends on a flat acknowledgement ("Right.").
4. THE PREMISE (character): the one idea, stated as if reading it off the card, then the follow-up question that treats it as data. "Listen, it's okay." is allowed here once.
5. THE MECHANISM THROUGH THE EXAMPLE (snap): the rule pushed to its limit inside the running example ("So the guy under the sink at midnight has no competition. Nobody else came.").
6. THE TRICK (snap): the concept's slogan rewritten as one of his questions, over the picture of someone finally doing the schlep; labels for the concept's own words.
7. THE VERDICT (character): the one number, said once, in the payoff of the running example; then the administrative exit. No new fact.
Keep the two hard rules from `/explain`: exactly one concrete number per video, and every line carries a distinct property (see "Say the number once"). The mechanism here is not a part he takes apart; it is a question pushed until the example answers it.

## Registers (--register)
- matan-deadpan (default): the interview register. Absurd questions asked straight, flat acknowledgements, one callback, the administrative exit. Hands still, the card in one hand.
- matan-gotcha: the "so you're saying" register. He restates the viewer's position in slightly worse words each time until it is the verdict; the snaps show the restatement getting worse. Use for misconceptions.
- matan-game: the prop register. He holds up printed photos or an object and rates or sorts them ("I have a little game I want to play."); the snaps are the things on the table. Use for choices between options. The prop must be an object with no writing on it.

## Tiers (--tier)
- `verdict` (like JJK `technique`): hook, wrong way, premise, mechanism, trick, verdict. Six shots. For one idea.
- `playbook` (like `domain`): adds a second snap after the trick that shows the idea scaling (three places, three beats). Seven shots.
- `full`: adds a second word slam before the verdict (the non-sequitur question as a card: "BOSCH OR WHIRLPOOL"). Eight shots.
Vocabulary: the guest = the viewer; the card = the question; the next guest = the exit.

## script.json shape
Identical to `/explain` plus `"style": "matan"` at the top level (the renderer defaults to `jjk`, so this field is mandatory here).
```json
{
  "style": "matan",
  "topic": "string",
  "slug": "kebab",
  "tier": "verdict|playbook|full",
  "register": "matan-deadpan|matan-gotcha|matan-game",
  "cast": "the young-man paragraph below, verbatim",
  "scenes": [
    {"id": "s1", "kind": "title", "kanji": "SCHLEP BLINDNESS", "english": "The good ideas are gross", "narration": "", "sound": "quiet studio room tone, no music"},
    {"id": "s2", "kind": "shot", "shot": "character", "narration": "[matan] Is a startup cooked if it involves touching a pipe? Serious question. This is called schlep blindness.", "keywords": ["cooked", "pipe", "schlep"], "video_prompt": "...", "sound": "quiet studio room tone, the faint hum of a microphone preamp, no music"},
    {"id": "s3", "kind": "shot", "shot": "snap", "narration": "[matan] Every guy in the coffee shop is building a recipe app. Meanwhile the sink is leaking. Right.", "labels": [{"text": "RECIPE APP", "cue": "recipe"}, {"text": "THE SCHLEP", "cue": "sink"}], "video_prompt": "...", "sound": "espresso machine, laptop keys, a slow drip, no music"}
  ]
}
```
Rules (unchanged from `/explain` unless noted):
- `narration` per scene: 1-3 short full sentences, at most 22 words on a character shot, 10-16 on a snap. Whole video 100-125 words, 45-60 s. One speaker: `[matan]` or untagged.
- `kind: title` scenes render locally (the word slam), no fal spend; 1.5 s, silent. `kanji` holds the English title in caps on one line (use " / " to stack two lines); `english` the subtitle. No CJK. Omit the title scene to skip the card.
- `video_prompt`: the framing, the gesture, the room, in the vocabulary below. On character shots paste the young-man paragraph verbatim (the renderer checks the first 40 characters of `cast`, "a teenage-looking young man with a big m"). On snap shots never mention him: the style keeps the cast paragraph off snap b-roll, and "no face near the camera, no readable text anywhere" belongs in every snap prompt. Do not write "no on-screen text" or "no dialogue" yourself; the renderer appends them. The renderer prepends the style lock and appends `Sound: <sound>`.
- `keywords` (optional, per scene): the words to paint yellow in the captions when they appear; without it the renderer highlights any number, else the longest non-stopword in each group.
- `labels` on snap shots: `[{"text": "THE SCHLEP", "cue": "sink"}]`; drawn by the renderer in white bold caps down the right edge the instant the cue word is spoken.
- Exactly one concrete number in the whole script, on the verdict beat, said once. "One" as in "one guy" does not count; "five minutes" does.
- Running example: a concrete everyday trade or place (a plumber, a laundromat, a moving company, a bike shop), never anything from the podcast, content or interview business, so the analogy and the lesson cannot be confused (see "No meta examples").

"Real video from a locked-off camera on a tripod, flat even lighting, plain unsaturated colour, sharp and clean, documentary-plain, no film grain, no anamorphic flare, no cinematic grading. "
The interview framing (the table, the bedsheet, the card, the boom mic) lives in the cast paragraph and in the renderer's `say_lines` on character shots only; when a talking head lived in a lock, every snap opened on a stranger's face before cutting to the b-roll.
Not cinematic and not a phone: a locked-off camera at eye level, flat light, nothing moves but him. Snaps are plain documentary b-roll of the running example in the same flat, unsaturated look; no slow motion, no drone shots, no lens flare.
Settings: his studio (the bedsheet, the black tables, the boom arms; one masked co-host at a third table is optional and never speaks); the snap locations belong to the running example (the coffee shop, the kitchen under the sink, the back office).
Camera grammar:
- title: handled locally, no prompt.
- character shots: one locked-off camera at eye level, medium shot, him at the table slightly left of centre, the card in one hand; one small action per shot (a glance down at the card, a slow nod, both hands flat on the table, holding a prop up), never a smile, a flat look into the lens to end the last shot.
- snap shots: 3-6 s of documentary b-roll that answers the line: a row of laptops in a coffee shop with a sink overflowing behind them; a man on his back under a kitchen sink at night; two hands sorting a pile of paper while everyone else leaves. No face near the camera; hands and backs are fine.
- the number: the payoff of the running example shown physically (every door on a block, one van outside), the label popping on the cue word.

## Captions (the renderer draws them; nothing to prompt)
The style's `captions` mode is `words`: the line is cut into groups of two to three words (breaking at punctuation), drawn in bold white caps with a thick black outline, centred just below the middle of the frame, one word per group in yellow, each group held until the next begins. Timing comes from Whisper on each shot's own audio (the clip for lip-synced shots, the dubbed wav for snaps). His clip channels caption in the shorts convention; his own uploads add small white pill labels over people, which is what the renderer's labels stand in for. Write lines that survive being read three words at a time: no subordinate clauses longer than a group, the question word first, the verdict word last in its sentence so it lands on its own card.

## Snap rules (cut to the picture the instant it is named)
- A snap is `"shot": "snap"`: rendered on turbo with no character refs, dubbed with the style's Fish clone, and cut to the length of its line (the model renders its 5 s minimum; the renderer trims the scene to the dub plus a 0.3 s tail, never under 2.5 s). Keep snap lines to 10-16 words, so the shot runs 3-6 s.
- The line names the thing first, the picture is that thing: write the prompt as the "after" state of the sentence ("a man on his back under a kitchen sink at night"), not a description of him.
- `labels` for anything enumerated, one per named item, cue on the word that names it. `"image"` (a fetched photo of the real thing as first frame) keeps the shape true on turbo; `"objects"` refs do the same with more control but move the shot to the reference model.
- Never ask the model for text; signs are blank, forms are blank paper. Labels and captions are drawn by the renderer.
- Snaps alternate with character shots. Never two character shots back to back; two snaps in a row are fine when both are short.

## Lean-mode rules
- At most 22 words and 3 sentences per character shot; the reference-audio window is 2-15 s.
- The character shot is phrased "the young man sits low at the black folding table with the yellow index card in one hand, glances down at the card, then looks straight into the camera, deadpan, no smile, hands still, and says, in the voice of Audio 1, exactly these words"; he addresses the lens as if it were the guest.
- Snap shots are off-screen by construction (work shots): Fish dubs the line, nobody on screen mouths it, so keep faces away from the camera in snap prompts.
- Title cards are silent.

## Lesson ideas (concept; running example; the one number; the exit)
- Schlep blindness: a plumber under the sink at midnight vs. a coffee shop of recipe apps; every pipe on the block clogs; "That's it. I have to film with the plumber in five minutes."
- Sunk cost: a laundromat owner feeding a broken dryer; the dryer ate three months of quarters; "So you're saying the dryer is the landlord now."
- Opportunity cost: a moving company that takes every job; each cheap job is one lost big job; "But congratulations."
- Survivorship bias: the bike shops still open on the street vs. the ones that closed; nine of ten closed; "Is it true that the closed ones just didn't want it enough?"
- Compound interest: a bakery that saves one tray a day; the trays double every ten years; "Isn't the max level just not selling any bread?"

## Narration is not stage direction (applies to every style)
The spoken line carries the idea: the rule, the condition, the cost, the number, the consequence, or something one person says to another. The `video_prompt` carries the action. Never narrate what the camera shows ("he looks at the card", "the plumber crawls under the sink", "cut to the coffee shop"): the viewer can see it, and spoken shot description sounds like a prompt being read aloud. Test before rendering: if a narration sentence could be pasted into `video_prompt` unchanged, cut it or turn it into a claim. Lines phrased as questions to the viewer are the surest way to stay on the right side of this.

## Object references (complicated shapes must be right)
If the concept is, or hinges on, a specific physical object with a complicated shape (a particular sneaker, a drive, an engine, a branded product), the video model will not get it from words. Before writing `script.json`, fetch 2-3 clean reference images (WebSearch/WebFetch, a product page, a Wikipedia diagram), crop them to 16:9 1280x720 with no captions or watermarks, save them under `<workspace>/assets/ref/objects/<object-slug>/`, and put them on the snap that shows the object:
```json
"objects": {"assets/ref/objects/plumber/plumber-1.jpg": "a man on his back under a kitchen sink, only his legs showing"}
```
The renderer appends them after the character stills (nine images in all) and tells the model to reproduce the object's exact shape. A snap with only object references still routes to the reference model; a fetched photo as `"image"` keeps it on turbo.

## Say the number once; every other line is a different property
The one concrete number is the cause, not the takeaway. State it once, on the number beat, and never again. Repeating the MAIN POINT in the verdict is fine and good; what must not be repeated is a figure that means nothing on its own. Every other spoken line must carry a distinct property or consequence: for schlep blindness, everyone builds the easy thing (shot 3), the brain hides the gross idea (shot 4), the gross idea has no competition (shot 5), the trick is to ask what you wish someone else would do (shot 6), the payoff is every door on the block (shot 7). Before rendering, list each shot's line beside the property it states; if two shots state the same thing, rewrite one.

## No meta examples: the running example is a concrete trade, never the craft itself
When the concept is about business, ideas, attention or persuasion, the running example must be a concrete, everyday trade or place (a plumber, a laundromat, a moving company, a bike shop), never anything from the podcast, content, interview or streaming business. A same-craft example makes the viewer lose track of what is the lesson and what is the illustration. Test: could a twelve-year-old say which sentence is about the plumber and which is the teacher's point? If not, change the trade.

## Full sentences, five ideas, one thread
- Full sentences, short: 3 to 12 words each, at most 3 per shot, every one ending in a full stop or a question mark. Fragments are allowed only as his acknowledgement or exit ("Right." "That's it.") and only after a full sentence in the same shot; never a shot made of fragments alone.
- At most five ideas per video, one per shot. The last shot carries only the payoff of the running example and the exit; it introduces no new fact.
- One thread: the running example is named in at least every other shot, so the exit lands on something the viewer has been holding.
- The payoff states the consequence in words, never a pose.

## Two kinds of shots: character shots and snaps, interleaved
Every shot is one of two kinds, set per scene with `"shot": "character" | "snap"` (`"work"` still works and means a snap that is not trimmed).
- SNAP shot: no face. The picture the line names: documentary b-roll of the running example, a side-by-side, a thing on the table. Turbo ($0.025/s), the line dubbed by the Fish clone, trimmed to the line. Labels pop on cue words. His voice and his captions carry the persona.
- CHARACTER shot: the face is on screen and he speaks the line. Reference stills, the voice sample, the model's own lip-synced speech, reference-to-video ($0.05/s at 480P).
- THE LIKENESS RULE: never lip-sync a person who is not clearly the character. If the likeness does not hold on a test shot, the shot becomes a snap: documentary footage that fits the line while his voice plays. The default stills held on the first render (Schlep Blindness, three character shots: the hair, the round face, the black jacket, the studio all read as him on the contact sheet); the user's verdict is still pending.
- Shot mix: half the shots are visuals; three character shots and three snaps, interleaved, is the normal shape. Never two character shots in a row.
- Cost: three snaps of 5-6 s plus three character shots of 6-8 s is about $2.5 at 480P regular price.
Before rendering, show the user the shot list with each shot's kind and the estimated cost, and ask which shots (if any) should change kind; they may flip any.

## The six-shot arc for this style
Six shots after the optional word slam, three of him and three snaps, interleaved:
1. CHARACTER, hook: the absurd premise as a question, the running example inside it, the concept named flat. Thumbnail candidate.
2. SNAP, the wrong way: what everyone does instead, shown; labels for what is being avoided; a flat "Right." to end.
3. CHARACTER, the premise: the one idea read off the card, then the follow-up that treats it as data.
4. SNAP, the mechanism: the rule pushed to its limit inside the running example; the picture is the limit.
5. SNAP, the trick: the concept's slogan as one of his questions; labels for the concept's own words; the picture is someone finally doing the schlep.
6. CHARACTER, the verdict: the one number in the payoff, then the administrative exit. Source 2-3 real images of any object that must look right; name objects literally in prompts; character shots carry likeness and voice, snaps carry the explanation.

## Never lip-sync a stranger
A character shot is allowed only when the reference stills reproduce the person. Check the rendered clip against the stills; if the model produced a generic look-alike, do not keep it: convert the shot to a dubbed work/snap shot (documentary footage of the thing being talked about, no prominent face) with the character's voice over it, and re-render on turbo. A stranger lip-syncing the character's lines is worse than no face at all. When a style's stills are soft (vertical phone crops, low resolution), default to zero or one character shot until sharper stills are sourced, and let the voice, captions and cutaways carry the video.

## Visualizing the words (why b-roll goes random, and the fix)
B-roll goes wrong in two ways: a stranger talking at the camera while the character's voice plays, and garbled text on things. The fix is what the picture shows, not a fixed prop:
- VISUALIZE THE VERB, NOT THE NOUN. A line is a claim about something happening; the picture shows that happening. "Everyone is building a recipe app" is a row of people typing in a coffee shop while a sink overflows behind them. "Nobody else came" is one man alone under a sink at night with the street empty outside. Write each snap as the consequence the line states, then check: would a viewer with the sound off understand the claim?
- NO WRITING ON PROPS, NOBODY TALKING. Never ask for signs, forms with text, numbers, screens with text or price tags; the model garbles them. Anything written becomes a renderer `"labels"` entry. On dubbed shots nobody speaks: people do the thing (type, crawl, sort, carry, leave) with mouths closed and never facing the camera, or the model turns them into random people talking over the voice-over.
- CONTINUITY, LIGHTLY. Keep the palette, time of day and place consistent unless the story moves, and write the move. When a story genuinely tracks one object (a van, a dryer, a sink), a script-level `"prop"` paragraph (+ `"prop_words"`) makes the renderer paste the same description into every prompt that names it, the way `cast` works; use it for that case, and do not force every shot onto one object when the line is about people or a place.
- SEED WHAT MATTERS. Source a real image for anything whose exact look carries the meaning (the "Object references" rule) and use it as `"image"` or `"objects"`.

## Dose the persona (the checklist is not the voice)
The quirk budget is a ceiling, not a quota. The absurd hook is structural; after it, one or two of his moves per video, placed where they land on their own: the flat restatement after the wrong way, the administrative exit at the end. Five quirks in sixty seconds (a non-sequitur, a callback, "but congratulations", "listen, it's okay" and an exit all in a row) read as a bit being performed. If a line would work without the quirk, leave the quirk out. He is funny because he never signals that anything is funny.

## He is on screen
The face is the continuity of the short. Three of six shots are him at the table (the hook, the premise in the middle, and the verdict), seeded from the sharpest stills; the snaps between them carry the visuals. Zero face is a fallback for a likeness that failed, not the default.

## No numbers, no text, in the footage (hard rule)
Video models cannot draw digits or words; every price tag, counter, sign, label, screen, chalkboard, form or handwritten number comes out garbled. Never write a prompt that asks for one, and never write a line whose visual would need one ("the invoice says", "a sign that reads..."). Show the quantity physically instead (every door on a block, one van, a pile of paper versus an empty desk) and put any words or numbers in renderer `"labels"` or captions. The index card in his hand is blank or turned away from the camera; never ask for writing on it. Pre-render check: grep the prompts for digits and for sign|price|menu|label|screen|text|number|written|chalk|form; any hit is rewritten.

## Fewer numbers
The exit carries the only number ("in five minutes") unless the payoff needs one more badly; never both. Where a rating would go, use a question instead ("Isn't the max level just no pipes?").

## Thumbnail
The poster is his face (the deadpan gets the clicks) with the video's phrase slammed across it in the caption look: script-level `"thumb": {"scene": "s2", "text": "IS IT COOKED IF IT TOUCHES A PIPE", "at": 3.0}`; the style default picks the first character shot. The text is the hook question or the exit, three to six words, caps.

## Which stills (decided)
Default refs are the channel stills: his mouth is visible and the studio reads as his. The interview stills (`iv-*.jpg`) put the big mic over his mouth in every frame, because the render copies the stills' composition; they stay on disk as a script-level `"refs"` override for a lesson that wants the exact interview frame. The user compared both renders and preferred the channel set.
