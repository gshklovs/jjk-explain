# /explain-clav bible

Sister to `/explain` (the JJK narrator), `/explain-iroh` (the tea master), `/explain-rick` (the garage rant), `/explain-stark` (the workshop test) and `/explain-hxh` (the freeze-frame narrator). Same pipeline, same script.json, a sixth temperature: a short. Where the narrator is cold and the inventor is mid-task, this voice is a twenty-year-old talking into his phone, deadpan, fast, faintly bored by how obvious the point is. He explains one idea with one blunt analogy, cuts to a picture the instant a thing is named, drops one number, and ends with a verdict from his own vocabulary. The teacher is Clavicular ("Clav"), the looksmaxxing streamer, as the two source clips show him: the door-to-door ad and the TV interview. That is the last time the name appears in this file. In cast paragraphs and video prompts he is "the young man"; never name him, the platforms, the interviewers or any brand.

The gold reference is the 92-second ad: a hook question in the first sentence, one analogy (door-to-door sales for clip distribution) carried all the way through, every mechanism beat staged as a scene inside the analogy (grandma pitching over the fence, a barber quoting mid-fade, a guy in Ohio at a red light), numbers dropped as fragments after a full sentence, one-word verdicts ("Brutal." "Billboard got mogged." "It's over for him."), and a hard cut to b-roll or a word slam roughly every eight seconds. Full transcript and frame notes: `docs/clav-research.md`.

## The teacher (how he teaches)
- He opens on the viewer's pain as a question, then answers it in the second sentence. "Tired of seeing the same clips on your feed every day? Tired of never being one of them? Bro, come meet the guys who deliver them."
- One analogy, chosen in the first ten seconds, carries the whole explanation. He never switches metaphors and never explains the analogy itself; he just lives inside it.
- The mechanism is a scene, not a definition. Instead of "distribution is parallel", he says "Grandma's pitching your app over the fence. A barber quoting your podcast mid-fade." Each scene is one concrete person doing one concrete thing, and that scene is the picture the video snaps to.
- Numbers land as fragments after a full sentence, said flat: "A hundred clips. Ten thousand doors. That was this morning." Then the sentence resumes.
- He echoes a phrase once, deadpan, with "dude": "Their own face, dude." "Mid-fade, dude." The echo is the joke; there is no other joke.
- The verdict is one word from his vocabulary, said as if reading a scoreboard: "Brutal." "Mogged." "Over." "Jester." Never a paragraph of praise or blame.
- He undercuts himself inside the pitch and keeps going: "I'm not gonna lie." "Beautiful billboard, I'm not gonna lie. But it just sits there."
- He talks to one person, "bro", never to an audience. Second person, present tense. "Your competitor opens his door. It's you again. Next morning, you again. By Friday, he's a user."
- The interview register is the same brain at half speed: a hedge ("like you said", "right?"), then the blunt line ("that's because they don't know what they're doing").
- He is never warm, never cruel, never impressed. Flat. The content is the outrage, the delivery is a shrug.
- He rates like a scoreboard and justifies like a lab: a decimal ("three point seven five", "ninety-four percent"), then the measurement behind it. When the viewer flinches, he corrects the scale ("five is average"), not the number.
- He argues by category. An objection is named ("one-off anecdote", "status mog, not a looks mog") and thereby dismissed; the skeptic is recruited into the rule ("by definition, you're one of us").
- One thing per video he refuses, on principle, in one sentence ("I wouldn't rate myself. Not responsible."). It is the only place he sounds careful, and it is funnier than the outrageous parts.

## Anchor lines (paraphrased from the clips; match the cadence, do not quote)
- "Tired of seeing the same thing on your feed every day? Tired of never being the thing?"
- "Bro, come meet the guys who actually deliver it."
- "Clippers with bags full of clips they cut ten minutes ago. Or creators filming with their own face. Their own face, dude."
- "Nobody's done door-to-door like this. I'm not gonna lie."
- "A hundred clips. Ten thousand doors. That was this morning."
- "Your competitor rented a billboard. Twenty grand. Brutal. Beautiful billboard, I'm not gonna lie. But it just sits there."
- "We knocked on two million doors. Billboard got mogged."
- "Your competitor opens his door. It's you again. Next morning, you again. By Friday, he's a user. It's over for him."
- "Some clips hit ten thousand doors. Some hit ten. That guy's a jester, bro. His problem, not yours."
- "Flops cost you nothing. Great deal, swear to God."
- Interview: "That's because they don't know what they're doing." "It can be as simple as losing weight, right?"
- Rating a host: "I won't be biased, you don't have to worry about that. Maybe a three point seven five. Mostly the interpupillary distance."
- Correcting the scale: "People don't understand the decimal scale. Five is average. Five is the fiftieth percentile."
- Granting the exception, then closing it: "That's a fair perspective. There's status mogs, money mogs. You might have me there. But it's not a looks mog."
- Refusing the anecdote: "Your bald fat buddy who gets laid is a one-off anecdote. We're talking general rules."
- Recruiting the skeptic: "You've done a calorie deficit. So by definition, you're a looksmaxxer. You're just on the surface level of it."
- The number with its reason: "There are no perfect tens because the technology isn't there yet. He's a ninety-four. Fixing the forehead ratio for six percent would be ridiculous."
- Declining on principle: "I wouldn't rate myself. I just don't think it's the responsible thing to do."
- Exit lines: "Go for it." "Who knows." "That's all I'm saying." "See you, bro. Everything's fine."

Quirks to keep (from twelve moments in `docs/clav-research.md`): every verdict is a number with decimals or a percentile and every number gets a measurement as its reason, never a feeling; he corrects the scale before the person; he grants the exception and closes it in the same breath; he refuses anecdotes by naming them; he declines exactly one thing per conversation, on principle; he recruits the skeptic into the category; escalation is calm (a wild claim, then the mechanism, then "that's all I'm saying"); exits are polite and final.

Cadence: 2.8 to 3.1 words per second, sentences of three to nine words, a full sentence then a fragment, "bro" or "dude" at the end of a line at most once per shot. Verdict words: brutal, mogged, over, jester, cooked, ascended, it's not over. Never "basically", never "so essentially", never a rhetorical "right?" more than once per video.

## Beats (a short, not a lecture)
1. TITLE (optional word slam): 1.5 s, the idea in white caps on black, a yellow one-line subtitle. "ZERO TO ONE" / "Competition is for losers". Silent. Leave it out of the script for no card at all.
2. HOOK (character): the viewer's pain as a question, the running example named in the same breath, the concept's name dropped flat. Face on screen, lip-synced.
3. THE ANALOGY (snap): the wrong way, shown. Cut to the picture the instant it is named; two or three labels pop as he lists what is wrong. The verdict word ends the shot.
4. THE MECHANISM THROUGH THE ANALOGY (character): the one idea that makes the right way work, as a scene inside the running example ("Night nurses are starving before sunrise, and nobody feeds them.").
5. THE RIGHT WAY (snap): the running example done right, shown; labels for the concept's own words.
6. THE NUMBER (snap): the one concrete number, said once, over a picture that makes it visible (the two things side by side). This is the only figure in the video.
7. THE VERDICT (character): how it scales and the one-word verdict, to the lens, then a flat stare for the still frame. No new fact.
Keep the two hard rules from `/explain`: exactly one concrete number per video, and every line carries a distinct property (see "Say the number once"). The mechanism here is not a part he takes apart; it is a scene inside the analogy that the snap shows.

## Registers (--register)
- clav-deadpan (default): the ad register. Fast, flat, second person, one analogy, one-word verdicts, one echo with "dude". Hand gestures small and few.
- clav-rant: the stream register. He is annoyed the point needs saying; sentences run into each other, "bro" twice, the analogy gets meaner (the competitor is a jester, the copy is cooked), the snaps come faster (two-second cuts). Use for misconceptions he has to demolish.
- clav-rating: the verdict register. He rates the alternatives on a scale like a scoreboard, one figure only (the one number), everything else "low", "mid", "mogged". The snaps are side-by-side comparisons with a label on each. Use for choices between options.

## Tiers (--tier)
- `verdict` (like JJK `technique`): hook, analogy, mechanism, number, verdict. Six shots. For one idea.
- `playbook` (like `domain`): adds a second right-way snap that shows the expansion (start small, then bigger). Seven shots.
- `full`: adds a second word slam before the number ("10X" / "or don't bother"). Eight shots.
Vocabulary: the feed = where the viewer lives; the competitor = the wrong way; the niche = where the right way starts; the verdict = the takeaway in one word.

## script.json shape
Identical to `/explain` plus `"style": "clav"` at the top level (the renderer defaults to `jjk`, so this field is mandatory here).
```json
{
  "style": "clav",
  "topic": "string",
  "slug": "kebab",
  "tier": "verdict|playbook|full",
  "register": "clav-deadpan|clav-rant|clav-rating",
  "cast": "the young-man paragraph below, verbatim",
  "scenes": [
    {"id": "s1", "kind": "title", "kanji": "ZERO TO ONE", "english": "Competition is for losers", "narration": "", "sound": "quiet room tone, no music"},
    {"id": "s2", "kind": "shot", "shot": "character", "narration": "[clav] Tired of opening another taco cart on the same street? That's one to n, bro. Copying. Competition is for losers.", "keywords": ["taco", "copying", "losers"], "video_prompt": "...", "sound": "quiet room tone, the faint hiss of a phone microphone, no music"},
    {"id": "s3", "kind": "shot", "shot": "snap", "narration": "[clav] Same tacos, same corner, everyone cutting the price. Nobody clears anything. Brutal.", "labels": [{"text": "1 TO N", "cue": "same"}, {"text": "PRICE WAR", "cue": "cutting"}], "video_prompt": "...", "sound": "street traffic, a griddle sizzle, no music"}
  ]
}
```
Rules (unchanged from `/explain` unless noted):
- `narration` per scene: 1-3 short full sentences, at most 22 words on a character shot, 10-16 on a snap. Whole video 100-125 words, 45-60 s. One speaker: `[clav]` or untagged.
- `kind: title` scenes render locally (the word slam), no fal spend; 1.5 s, silent. `kanji` holds the English title in caps on one line (use " / " to stack two lines); `english` the subtitle. No CJK. Omit the title scene to skip the card.
- `video_prompt`: the framing, the gesture, the room, in the vocabulary below. On character shots paste the young-man paragraph verbatim (the renderer checks the first 40 characters of `cast`, "a young man with dark wavy hair, a sharp"). On snap shots never mention him: the style keeps the cast paragraph off snap b-roll, and "no face near the camera, no readable text anywhere" belongs in every snap prompt. Do not write "no on-screen text" or "no dialogue" yourself; the renderer appends them. The renderer prepends the style lock and appends `Sound: <sound>`.
- `keywords` (optional, per scene): the words to paint yellow in the captions when they appear; without it the renderer highlights any number, else the longest non-stopword in each group.
- `labels` on snap shots: `[{"text": "PRICE WAR", "cue": "cutting"}]`; drawn by the renderer in white bold caps down the right edge the instant the cue word is spoken.
- Exactly one concrete number in the whole script, on the number beat, said once. "One" as in "zero to one" does not count; "ten times" does.
- Running example: a concrete everyday product (a taco cart, a sneaker, a gym, a barbershop), never anything from the content, marketing or streaming business, so the analogy and the lesson cannot be confused (see "No meta examples").

## Cast (use verbatim; never name him)
The young man: "a young man with dark wavy hair, a sharp jawline and a plain black T-shirt, centred in frame at chest height in front of a plain bedroom wall, lit by a ring light"
Alternates for the room only (keep the first clause identical): "... in front of a plain office wall, lit by a ring light"; "... on a daytime city sidewalk, phone held at arm's length".

## Style lock (the renderer prepends this; write prompts that agree with it)
"Real phone-shot 4K video, a vertical short reframed to 16:9, ring-light clean, shallow phone-portrait depth of field, slightly warm colour, sharp and clean, handheld-still, no film grain, no anamorphic flare, no cinematic grading. "
The talking-head framing (centred at chest height, straight into the lens, ring light in the eyes) is added by the renderer on character shots only, through `say_lines`; when it lived in the lock, every snap opened on a stranger's face before cutting to the b-roll.
Not cinematic: no anamorphic flare, no film grain, no teal-and-orange. A phone on a tripod, a ring light, a wall. Snaps are phone b-roll, a screenshot-like card, a meme-like still or a side-by-side, in the same warm clean phone look.
Settings: the wall (bedroom or office, one plant or a shelf at most); the sidewalk (daytime, cars soft behind); the snap locations belong to the running example (the street corner, the hospital doors, the cart counter).
Optional flavour, one video at a time: the 90s-office skit look of the ad (CRT monitors, a plaid shirt and a lanyard, VHS date stamp), written into every prompt of that video and into the cast paragraph's clothes; the stills carry that shirt already.
Camera grammar:
- title: handled locally, no prompt.
- character shots: phone on a tripod at chest height, bust framing, him centred, straight to the lens, one small gesture per shot (an open hand, a finger count, a temple tap, a slow blink), a flat stare to end the last shot.
- snap shots: 3-6 s of b-roll or a card that answers the line: a push along a row of identical carts; the hospital doors at blue hour; a split screen of the two options; a screenshot-like card with a blank headline bar. No face near the camera; hands and backs are fine.
- the number: a side-by-side, the better thing on the right, the label popping on the cue word.

## Captions (the renderer draws them; nothing to prompt)
The style's `captions` mode is `words`: the line is cut into groups of two to four words (breaking at punctuation), drawn in bold white caps with a thick black outline, centred just below the middle of the frame, one word per group in yellow, each group held until the next begins. Timing comes from Whisper on each shot's own audio (the clip for lip-synced shots, the dubbed wav for snaps). The measured rhythm of the ad is 3-8 words per caption every 1-2.4 s; the shorts convention is shorter, so the renderer packs at most four. Write lines that survive being read four words at a time: no subordinate clauses longer than a group, the verdict word last in its sentence so it lands on its own card.

## Snap rules (cut to the picture the instant it is named)
- A snap is `"shot": "snap"`: rendered on turbo with no character refs, dubbed with the style's Fish voice, and cut to the length of its line (the model renders its 5 s minimum; the renderer trims the scene to the dub plus a 0.3 s tail, never under 2.5 s). Keep snap lines to 10-16 words, so the shot runs 3-6 s.
- The line names the thing first, the picture is that thing: write the prompt as the "after" state of the sentence ("a row of identical taco carts"), not a description of him.
- `labels` for anything enumerated, one per named item, cue on the word that names it. `"image"` (a fetched photo of the real thing as first frame) keeps the shape true on turbo; `"objects"` refs do the same with more control but move the shot to the reference model.
- Never ask the model for text; signs are blank, headlines are bars. Labels and captions are drawn by the renderer.
- Snaps alternate with character shots. Never two character shots back to back; two snaps in a row are fine when both are short.

## Sound line
H3-Max generates native audio. Ask for the room and the b-roll only: "quiet room tone", "the faint hiss of a phone microphone", "street traffic", "a griddle sizzle", "a sliding door", "a vending machine thunk", "foil crinkling", "a keyboard", "a car passing". Always end with "no music" unless the script sets a `"music"` bed; the style has none by default.

## Reference stills and voice (default on)
The style sets `"refs"` to five stills cut from the two clips (`assets/ref/clav/clav-face.jpg`, `clav-bust.jpg`, `clav-hands.jpg`, `clav-3q.jpg`, `clav-wide.jpg`) with `"seed": 1217`, so every character shot uses the reference-to-video endpoint and he is the same man across cuts. The renderer prepends "Image 1 to Image N show the same young man; keep his face, dark wavy hair, jawline and build consistent with them; his clothes and the room follow the description." The four office stills wear the ad's plaid shirt; the cast paragraph's black T-shirt overrides in the prompt. Extras: `clav-face-2.jpg` (a second close-up), `clav-ref.mp4` (7 s of him talking with both hands, for `"ref_videos"`, untested). User stills go FIRST in a script-level `"refs"` as `user-<n>.jpg`. `"refs": []` renders text-only. Snap shots send no refs.
Voice: lean mode by default; `assets/ref/clav/clav-voice.wav` (14 s cut from the interview clip) is the sample the model speaks from on character shots. Snap shots are dubbed by the style's Fish voice: a private clone made from that same wav (`8a50551a527348a78507cb83fa300773`), overridable with `CLAV_FISH_VOICE_ID` (public "clav" voices exist on fish.audio; ids in `docs/clav-research.md`).

## Lean-mode rules
- At most 22 words and 3 sentences per character shot; the reference-audio window is 2-15 s.
- The character shot is phrased "the young man is centred in frame at chest height with a ring light catch in his eyes, looks straight into the phone camera, deadpan, small hand gestures, and says, in the voice of Audio 1, exactly these words"; he does look at the lens in this style, so do not fight it in the prompt.
- Snap shots are off-screen by construction (work shots): Fish dubs the line, nobody on screen mouths it, so keep faces away from the camera in snap prompts.
- Title cards are silent.

## Lesson ideas (concept; running example; the one number; the verdict)
- Zero to one: a taco cart on a street of taco carts vs. the only cart at the hospital exit before sunrise; ten times better than the vending machine; "Copying is over, bro."
- Compound interest: a gym membership used twice a week vs. once a month; the money doubles every ten years; "Consistency mogs talent."
- Opportunity cost: a barbershop that takes walk-ins vs. one that books; every walk-in costs one booked cut; "The free chair isn't free."
- Survivorship bias: the sneaker brands you can name vs. the ones that folded the same year; nine in ten never made it; "You only see the winners, bro."
- Pareto: a bakery's forty products vs. the four that sell; four items make eighty percent; "Cut the menu. It's over for the croissant."

## Narration is not stage direction (applies to every style)
The spoken line carries the idea: the rule, the condition, the cost, the number, the consequence, or something one person says to another. The `video_prompt` carries the action. Never narrate what the camera shows ("he looks at the camera", "the carts line up", "cut to the hospital"): the viewer can see it, and spoken shot description sounds like a prompt being read aloud. Test before rendering: if a narration sentence could be pasted into `video_prompt` unchanged, cut it or turn it into a claim. Lines addressed to "bro" are the surest way to stay on the right side of this.

## Object references (complicated shapes must be right)
If the concept is, or hinges on, a specific physical object with a complicated shape (a particular sneaker, a drive, an engine, a branded product), the video model will not get it from words. Before writing `script.json`, fetch 2-3 clean reference images (WebSearch/WebFetch, a product page, a Wikipedia diagram), crop them to 16:9 1280x720 with no captions or watermarks, save them under `<workspace>/assets/ref/objects/<object-slug>/`, and put them on the snap that shows the object:
```json
"objects": {"assets/ref/objects/sneaker/side.jpg": "the sneaker's exact silhouette and sole"}
```
The renderer appends them after the character stills (nine images in all) and tells the model to reproduce the object's exact shape. A snap with only object references still routes to the reference model; a fetched photo as `"image"` keeps it on turbo.

## Say the number once; every other line is a different property
The one concrete number is the cause, not the takeaway. State it once, on the number beat, and never again. Repeating the MAIN POINT in the verdict is fine and good; what must not be repeated is a figure that means nothing on its own. Every other spoken line must carry a distinct property or consequence: for zero to one, copying starts a price war (shot 3), the secret is a need nobody serves (shot 4), the niche has no competitor (shot 5), the bar is ten times better (shot 6), the monopoly expands from small (shot 7). Before rendering, list each shot's line beside the property it states; if two shots state the same thing, rewrite one.

## No meta examples: the running example is a concrete product, never the craft itself
When the concept is about business, marketing, attention or persuasion, the running example must be a concrete, everyday product or place (a taco cart, a gym, a barbershop, a bakery), never anything from the content, clipping, streaming or marketing business. A same-craft example makes the viewer lose track of what is the lesson and what is the illustration. Test: could a twelve-year-old say which sentence is about the product and which is the teacher's point? If not, change the product.

## Full sentences, five ideas, one thread
- Full sentences, short: 3 to 12 words each, at most 3 per shot, every one ending in a full stop. Fragments are allowed only as his number-drop or verdict ("Twenty grand. Brutal.") and only after a full sentence in the same shot; never a shot made of fragments alone.
- At most five ideas per video, one per shot. The last shot carries only the payoff of the running example and the verdict; it introduces no new fact.
- One thread: the running example is named in at least every other shot, so the verdict lands on something the viewer has been holding.
- The payoff states the consequence in words, never a pose.

## Two kinds of shots: character shots and snap shots (interleave them)
Every shot is one of two kinds, set per scene with `"shot": "character" | "snap"` (`"work"` still works and means a snap that is not trimmed).
- CHARACTER shot: the face is on screen and he speaks the line. Reference stills, the voice sample, the model's own lip-synced speech, reference-to-video ($0.05/s at 480P). Use it for the hook, the mechanism and the verdict.
- SNAP shot: no face. The picture the line names: b-roll of the running example, a side-by-side, a screenshot-like card. Turbo ($0.025/s), the line dubbed by the Fish clone, trimmed to the line. Labels pop on cue words.
- Interleave: three of each in a six-shot short, never two character shots in a row.
- Cost: three character shots of 8-10 s plus three snaps is about $2.50 at 480P regular price.
Before rendering, show the user the shot list with each shot's kind and the estimated cost, and ask which shots should be character (lip-synced) and which snap (dubbed); they may flip any.

## The six-shot arc for this style
Six shots after the optional word slam: three character, three snap, alternating:
1. CHARACTER, hook: the question, the running example, the concept named flat.
2. SNAP, the wrong way: the analogy's failure shown, labels for what is wrong, the verdict word.
3. CHARACTER, the mechanism: the one idea, as a scene inside the example.
4. SNAP, the right way: the example done right, labels for the concept's own words.
5. SNAP, the number: the one figure over a side-by-side.
6. CHARACTER, the verdict: how it scales, the one-word verdict, the flat stare.
Two snaps in a row (4 and 5) are fine because both are short. Source 2-3 real images of any object that must look right; name objects literally in prompts; character shots carry likeness and voice, snaps carry the explanation.

## Never lip-sync a stranger
A character shot is allowed only when the reference stills reproduce the person. Check the rendered clip against the stills; if the model produced a generic look-alike, do not keep it: convert the shot to a dubbed work/snap shot (stock-style footage of the thing being talked about, no prominent face) with the character's voice over it, and re-render on turbo. A stranger lip-syncing the character's lines is worse than no face at all. When a style's stills are soft (vertical phone crops, low resolution), default to zero or one character shot until sharper stills are sourced, and let the voice, captions and cutaways carry the video.

## Visualizing the words (why b-roll goes random, and the fix)
B-roll goes wrong in two ways: a stranger talking at the camera while the character's voice plays, and garbled text on things. The fix is what the picture shows, not a fixed prop:
- VISUALIZE THE VERB, NOT THE NOUN. A line is a claim about something happening; the picture shows that happening. "Nobody clears anything" is carts with no customers and food going cold. "Night nurses are starving" is tired staff in scrubs walking out of the hospital before dawn, one asleep in the break room, then the same nurse finally eating. Write each snap as the consequence the line states, then check: would a viewer with the sound off understand the claim?
- NO WRITING ON PROPS, NOBODY TALKING. Never ask for signs, menus, numbers, screens with text or price tags; the model garbles them. Anything written becomes a renderer `"labels"` entry. On dubbed shots nobody speaks: people do the thing (eat, queue, walk, work, sleep) with mouths closed and never facing the camera, or the model turns them into random people talking over the voice-over.
- CONTINUITY, LIGHTLY. Keep the palette, time of day and place consistent unless the story moves, and write the move. When a story genuinely tracks one object (a cart, a disc, a can), a script-level `"prop"` paragraph (+ `"prop_words"`) makes the renderer paste the same description into every prompt that names it, the way `cast` works; use it for that case, and do not force every shot onto one object when the line is about people or a place.
- SEED WHAT MATTERS. Source a real image for anything whose exact look carries the meaning (the "Object references" rule) and use it as `"image"` or `"objects"`.
